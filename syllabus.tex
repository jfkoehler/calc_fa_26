\section{Course Description}
% =========================================================

Calculus is a way of reasoning about accumulation, change, dynamics,
and form. This course follows the historical and conceptual development
of these ideas from problems of measurement, quadrature, and motion to
differential equations, high-dimensional optimization, and contemporary
artificial intelligence. Mathematics is studied alongside computation,
visual culture, modeling, history and sociology of science, and critical
studies of data and AI.

Rather than treating history or culture as decoration around a fixed
mathematical sequence, the course takes what Otto Toeplitz called a
\emph{genetic approach}: problems and practices that motivated
mathematical ideas help introduce those ideas. Students will move
repeatedly among concrete problems, finite constructions, computational
experiments, visualizations, formal mathematics, models, and critical
interpretation.

The course begins with integration rather than differentiation. This
order reflects both the historical development of calculus and the
course's emphasis on measurement and accumulation. Problems of area,
volume, summation, and infinity provide a route into the integral before
we turn to motion, tangency, and the derivative. These two traditions
eventually meet in the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

\section{Guiding Questions}
% =========================================================

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]

\item How can finite procedures be used to describe continuous quantities?

\item How do approximation and limiting processes produce mathematical
      knowledge?

\item How do we represent and measure change?

\item What can mathematical models reveal, and what do they conceal?

\item How did calculus become a technology of prediction, optimization,
      and control?

\item What happens when people, images, language, and intelligence become
      mathematical representations?

\end{itemize}

\section{Calculus Colloquium}
% =========================================================

Throughout the semester students will open selected classes with short
scholarly presentations extending the current material. Each colloquium
contribution consists of a \textbf{three-page LaTeX brief},
\textbf{five slides}, and a \textbf{5--7 minute presentation} followed
by discussion.

Topics may include an additional reading, historical episode,
mathematical problem, visual object, model, computational experiment,
or contemporary application. Across successive rounds the emphasis
will move from \textbf{explanation} to \textbf{analysis} to
\textbf{synthesis}. Selected work will be revised after feedback.

The Colloquium is intended to develop mathematical communication as
well as mathematical knowledge: students will practice explaining
technical ideas precisely, constructing mathematical arguments,
designing effective visual representations, and presenting technical
work to an interdisciplinary audience.

\section{Projects}
% =========================================================

\textbf{Project I: Modeling, Measurement \& Representation.}
A midsemester investigation combining calculus, data or observation,
visualization, computation, and interpretation. Students will formulate
a question, determine what can be measured or represented
mathematically, construct an analysis, and examine the assumptions
involved in that representation.

\medskip

\textbf{Final Project: Calculus in the World.}
A sustained mathematical and computational investigation connecting
calculus to a historical, cultural, scientific, social, technological,
or artistic question. Projects will culminate in a written technical
report and presentation at the final symposium.

\section{Readings and Seminar}
% =========================================================

Calculus has a history. Its concepts emerged from particular problems,
arguments, instruments, institutions, and disagreements about what
constitutes mathematical knowledge. We will therefore read mathematics
alongside texts from the history and sociology of science, science and
technology studies, visual culture, cybernetics, data studies, and
critical studies of artificial intelligence.

Historical primary sources will include selections from Archimedes,
Bonaventura Cavalieri, Isaac Barrow, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz,
Jean le Rond d'Alembert, Leonhard Euler, Daniel Bernoulli, and
Gaspard Monge. Many of these readings will be drawn from Dirk
Struik's \emph{A Source Book in Mathematics, 1200--1800}.

Other readings will include selections from Otto Toeplitz,
Michel Serres, Gilles Deleuze and F\'elix Guattari, Theodore Porter,
Lorraine Daston and Peter Galison, Norbert Wiener, Andrew Pickering,
Geoffrey Bowker and Susan Leigh Star, Trevor Paglen, and Kate Crawford,
together with primary sources from the history of neural networks and
machine learning.

These texts are not assigned as authorities to be accepted. Students
will be asked to reconstruct arguments, identify assumptions, compare
different forms of evidence, and evaluate claims in relation to the
mathematics we are studying.

\section{Working Practices}
% =========================================================

Mathematics in this course will be developed through several different
forms of work: calculation by hand, drawing and visualization,
computational experimentation, mathematical argument, technical writing,
historical reading, and discussion.

Students will use computational tools to investigate patterns, test
conjectures, visualize mathematical objects, simulate models, and work
with data. Depending on the problem, appropriate tools may include
Jupyter and Python, spreadsheets, Desmos, GeoGebra, computer algebra
systems, or other mathematical software. Students are expected to
document computational experiments sufficiently for another person to
understand and reproduce them.

Students will also learn to use \LaTeX{} for mathematical and technical
writing. Templates and examples will be provided, and expectations will
develop over the course of the semester.


% =========================================================
\section{Generative Artificial Intelligence}
% =========================================================

Generative AI is part of the subject matter of this course, but it is
not initially permitted as a tool for completing coursework.

During the semester we will develop the mathematical ideas needed to
understand neural networks, loss functions, gradient-based learning,
backpropagation, representation, and related computational systems.
We will also examine these technologies historically and critically.

Later in the semester, the class will reconsider when and how
generative AI systems may appropriately be used in coursework. Any
change in this policy will be announced explicitly. Until then,
generative AI systems should not be used to generate mathematical
solutions, explanations, code, reading responses, or written work
submitted for the course.

The governing principle is that we will work toward understanding these
systems before relying on them as mathematical tools.
